Six accents, one ledger.

Identical structure, identical type, identical data — judge each on the one job the accent has to do: making overdue and not sent findable in a column of forty rows without reading a word.

Each block shows the accent on its operating ground, then the same accent on the ink ground below it, since the ledger and analytics surfaces will live there. One deliberate difference: the two merged candidates lead on collected rather than outstanding — that flip is the point of the merge, so it's shown rather than held back.

E — THE MERGE · RECOMMENDED

Green for what's done. One warm accent for what needs you.

This is the merge you asked for, and it's stronger than any single accent — because the two things a bursar reads are how much has come in and what's still broken, and those deserve different colours. Ink carries everything neutral, so the palette is still only ever three inks deep on screen.

The discipline that keeps it from sliding back into badge soup: green only ever marks money received or a settled row — never a button, never a heading, never a chart axis. The warm accent only ever marks something a human must act on. Two variants below differ only in which warm accent partners the green.

Watch out: ochre is a quieter alarm than red, so a genuinely urgent state — a failed payment provider, a destructive action — still needs true red as a third, rare voice. That's acceptable: it appears on maybe four screens.

What the merge buys

The headline figure flips from outstanding to collected — the number an owner actually wants — and arrears move to a supporting line. Same data, entirely different feeling.

The rule that protects it

Green is reserved for received money and settled rows. It never becomes a primary button, a link, a nav highlight or a chart series. The moment it does, the system is gone.

Buttons stay ink

Primary actions are solid ink, not green and not accent — which is what stops this reading like every other dashboard. The accent is information, never furniture.

My pick

E2. It keeps the distinctiveness of ochre, gains the warmth of green on the number people care about, and doesn't fail one user in twelve. E1 is the more traditional and the more brutal.

HOW TO JUDGE

Squint at each block

Whichever one still shows you exactly two flagged rows when the text goes blurry is doing its job. That's the whole test.

One accent or two

A and C spend one colour on everything. B and D need a second for alarm — more flexible, more prone to drifting back into badge soup.

What the accent praises

A and C colour the problem. D colours the progress. That choice sets the product's tone more than the hue does.

My ranking

E2, then C, then A. The merge wins because it colours both halves of the job; ochre alone is the strongest single accent; red alone is the most forceful and the most severe. Cobalt is the safe answer and the crowded one.
